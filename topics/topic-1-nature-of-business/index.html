Chapter 1 1.1 The nature of a business

A business meets needs and wants

  • A need is something people must have, such as food. A want is something they would like, such as a flavoured drink.
  • The business provides a good, a service, or both.
  • Not every business’s main aim is profit. Charities and some clubs exist to serve members or the community.
  • In Australia, businesses also provide millions of jobs.

A business is an organisation that attempts to satisfy the needs and wants of the community by providing goods and/or services.

Chapter 1 1.2 Producing goods and services

Inputs go in. Value comes out.

  • Inputs are the resources: labour, finance, equipment, raw materials.
  • People and machines transform those inputs.
  • Outputs are the finished goods or services.
  • Value adding means the output is worth more to the customer than the separate inputs. Flour and water become a loaf people will pay for.

Value adding has three parts: the inputs, the process, and why the output is worth more than those inputs on their own.

Chapter 1 1.3 Economic and social roles

Money effects and community effects

Economic role

The money side. Has wealth been created? Are more people employed? Are new products reaching more customers?

Social role

The community side. Are essential services provided? Is the business fair? Is the environmental impact acceptable?

Accountability

Managers are answerable to groups the business affects, such as owners, staff, customers and the community.

Ethics

Standards of right and wrong. A choice can be legal and still fail an ethical test if it harms people the business could have protected.

The roles of business are profit, employment, incomes, choice, innovation, entrepreneurship and risk, wealth, and quality of life.

Chapter 1 Profit

Profit keeps the doors open

Profit = revenue − expenses
Return on investment = profit ÷ money invested × 100

  • Revenue is money in (sales and fees). Expenses are costs (wages, rent, stock, power).
  • Profit pays day-to-day costs and gives the owner a return.
  • The same profit can be a better or worse result depending on how much money was invested.

Worked example. A juice bar invests $80,000 and makes $16,000 profit. Return = 16,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = 20%. A second shop invests $40,000 and also makes $16,000. Return = 40%. Same profit, higher return, because less money was used.

Chapter 1 Employment and incomes

Jobs, then pay

Employment

Owners often hire other people. Human skill turns inputs into goods and services. Providing jobs is a core role of business.

Pressure from global rivals has led some large firms to downsize (shrink the workforce). A job can end by redundancy when the work itself is no longer needed, often because technology or a restructure replaced it. Redundancy can be voluntary or involuntary.

Incomes

Staff are paid for their work. A wage is an hourly rate and may include overtime. That income lets people buy goods and services, which creates sales for other businesses.

Wages are an expense. More staff can mean better service and more sales, and also higher costs and a smaller profit.

Chapter 1 Choice and innovation

Rivals push businesses to improve

  • Choice: in a competitive market, many businesses offer similar products. Customers can compare price, quality and where to buy.
  • That pressure pushes businesses toward lower prices, better quality, and clearer promotion. A brand is the name, design or symbol that sets one product apart.
  • Innovation is a new product or a real improvement: better features, or a production method that uses fewer resources and makes more output.
  • Innovation helps a business keep a competitive advantage.

A phone that only made calls, then a smartphone that takes photos and pays at a counter, is innovation in the product. A bakery that automates mixing is innovation in the process.

Chapter 1 Entrepreneurship, wealth, quality of life

Risk, assets, and everyday life

Entrepreneurship and risk

An entrepreneur turns an idea into a business and accepts that it may fail. The risk is personal time and money. The reward, if it works, is profit and a business that did not exist before.

Wealth

For owners, success can raise the value of the business. In a public company, the share price and dividends (a share of profits paid to shareholders) can rise. For the community, tax on profits helps fund hospitals, schools, transport and emergency services.

Quality of life

New goods and services change daily life: safer food, medicines, easier communication. Research by businesses, universities and government is part of that improvement.

Wealth for the owner and wealth for the community are different. One is the value of the owner’s investment. The other is tax and jobs that the wider society can use.

1. Which statement best describes a business?

  1. An organisation owned by the government to provide essential services.
  2. An organisation that tries to satisfy needs and wants by providing goods and/or services.
  3. An organisation that must make a profit in its first year.
  4. A company whose shares are listed on the Australian Securities Exchange.

B. The definition is about satisfying needs and wants with goods and/or services. Profit, government ownership and a share-market listing are possible features of some businesses, not the definition of every business.

2. A café uses coffee beans, milk, a machine and a barista’s skill to sell a cup of coffee that customers value more than the separate ingredients. Which idea is this?

  1. Liquidation
  2. Value adding
  3. Redundancy
  4. A dividend

B. Inputs are combined into an output worth more to the customer. That is value adding. Liquidation is selling assets to pay debts. Redundancy is a job ending because the work is no longer needed. A dividend is profit paid to shareholders.

3. Two owners each make $16,000 profit. One invested $80,000. The other invested $40,000. Why is the second result stronger?

  1. The second business created more jobs.
  2. The second business has a higher return on investment.
  3. The second business must be a public company.
  4. Profit does not depend on expenses.

B. Return on investment = profit ÷ money invested × 100. That is 20% for the first owner and 40% for the second. The profit in dollars is the same. The return is higher because less money produced it.

4. Nora designs a reusable lunch box and spends her savings to start making it, knowing the product might not sell. Nora is best described as

  1. a creditor
  2. a receiver
  3. an entrepreneur
  4. an external stakeholder only

C. She has an idea and takes a personal risk to turn it into a business. A creditor is someone the business owes money to. A receiver is an outsider appointed when a business is in serious financial trouble.

Short answer and case study

5. Explain how profit and employment can support each other and also conflict. (4 marks)

1 mark. Profit is revenue minus expenses. It lets the business pay its costs and reward the owner.

1 mark. Employment is the business hiring people whose skills produce the goods or services.

1 mark. They support each other because staff help create the sales that lead to profit, and profit can pay for more staff.

1 mark. They can conflict because wages are an expense. Hiring more people raises costs and can reduce profit, so an owner may downsize even though jobs are a social role of business.

6. Case study. Lila opens “Platform Bowls”, a takeaway grain-bowl shop at Auburn station. She uses her $50,000 savings. In year 1, revenue is $180,000 and expenses are $165,000. She employs two casual staff. A rival opens nearby and Lila adds a gluten-free menu.

Identify the business roles shown, and calculate her profit and return on investment. (6 marks)

1 mark. Entrepreneurship and risk: Lila used her own savings on a new idea that might fail.

1 mark. Employment and incomes: two casual staff are hired and paid.

1 mark. Choice and innovation: the new gluten-free menu is a response to a rival and gives customers another option.

1 mark. Profit = 180,000 − 165,000 = $15,000.

1 mark. Return on investment = 15,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 30%.

1 mark. The 30% is the profit compared with the money she invested, not the revenue.

Sort every business four ways

Size

How many people it employs.

Geography

Local area, the whole country, or many countries.

Industry

What it does in the chain from raw materials to services.

Legal structure

A business has all four labels at once. A local café can be a small, tertiary, sole-trader business. Woolworths is large, national (and beyond), tertiary, and a public company.

Chapter 2 Size

Count the employees

  • Micro: fewer than 5 employees.
  • Small: fewer than 20. Micro businesses sit inside this band.
  • Medium: 20 to 199.
  • Large: 200 or more.
  • SME: small and medium together — under 200 employees.

These bands count employees. Some other organisations use revenue instead.

Chapter 2 Local, national, global

Where it operates

Local

Works in one area and sells to nearby customers. Many small businesses are local. Example: a bakery on one suburban street.

National

Operates across Australia. A national customer base can support bulk buying. Examples: Coles, Woolworths, Bunnings.

Global

Sells in many countries. Production may stay in one country while sales are worldwide. A transnational also produces in more than one country. Examples of global reach: Samsung, Toyota, Netflix.

Global and transnational are close, but not identical. Global often means the sales cross borders. Transnational means the business operates — produces and sells — in many countries.

Chapter 2 One Australian business goes global

Canva — designed in Australia, used worldwide

  • Canva was founded in Australia by Melanie Perkins, Cliff Obrecht and Cameron Adams. It is an online design tool.
  • The product is delivered over the internet, so customers outside Australia can use it without a shop in their suburb.
  • As it grew, Canva served individuals, schools and businesses in many countries and built a presence beyond Australia.
  • That is global expansion of an Australian business: an idea from Australia, then customers and operations in other countries.

Canva sells an online design tool. Customers in other countries use it through the internet. A bigger market also means competition from other global design tools.

Chapter 2 One global business enters Australia

Aldi — a global retailer comes here

  • Aldi is a German discount supermarket. It already operated in other countries before it entered Australia.
  • It opened its first Australian stores in 2001, beginning in Sydney, and then spread to other states.
  • It brought a low-price, smaller-range model into a grocery market dominated by large local chains.
  • Effect on Australian business: more competition on price, pressure on Coles and Woolworths, and another choice for shoppers.

Canva is an Australian business that expanded overseas. Aldi is a global business that expanded into Australia.

Chapter 2 Industry sectors

Five sectors, from the ground to the home

Primary

Takes natural resources. Farming, mining, fishing, forestry.

Secondary

Turns materials into finished goods. Manufacturing and construction.

Tertiary

Provides a service. Retail, transport, health, hairdressing.

Quaternary

Information services. Banking, media, telecommunications, IT.

Quinary

Services once done at home. Childcare, cleaning, takeaway food, home maintenance.

Quaternary and quinary grew out of the tertiary (service) sector. Quaternary transfers information. Quinary replaces work households used to do themselves.

Chapter 2 Why services grew

External change moved jobs toward services

  • Technology cut the number of people needed in some factories and created information jobs (software, data, online banking).
  • Trade made many manufactured goods cheaper to import, so some secondary work left Australia while retail and logistics grew.
  • Incomes and time: as households earned more, they paid businesses for meals, childcare and cleaning (quinary).
  • An older, more urban population needs health, education and personal services (tertiary and quaternary).

Technology, trade, higher incomes and an older urban population moved jobs toward tertiary, quaternary and quinary work.

Chapter 2 Legal structure at a glance

Who owns it, and who pays if it fails?

  • Unincorporated: the law treats the owner and the business as the same. Sole traders and partnerships. Unlimited liability.
  • Incorporated: the company is a separate legal entity. Private and public companies. Limited liability.
  • Five structures to compare: sole trader, partnership, private company, public company, and government enterprise.
  • Other structures are a company limited by guarantee, a cooperative, and a trust.
Chapter 2 Sole trader and partnership

One owner, or a few, with unlimited liability

Sole trader

One owner. Keeps all profits and makes all decisions. Money comes from the owner’s equity and from loans. Unlimited liability: if the business cannot pay, the owner’s personal assets can be sold. Simple to start. The owner and the business are the same legal person.

Partnership

More than one owner, usually 2 to 20 (some professions may have more under the Partnership Act 1892 NSW). A partnership agreement should cover ownership shares, how profits are split, and what happens if a partner leaves. Skills and money are shared. So are debts: unlimited liability. Disagreements can threaten the business.

Chapter 2 Private company and public company

A company is a separate legal person

Private company — Pty Ltd

1 to 50 shareholders. Separate legal entity. Limited liability: shareholders can lose their investment, not (in the ordinary case) their personal house, for company debts. Directors run the longer-term decisions and are chosen by shareholders. Set up under the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) and registered with ASIC. “Proprietary” means shares are not offered freely to the public. “Limited” means limited liability.

Public company — Ltd

A company the public can own, usually by buying shares on the ASX. At least 5 shareholders and no set maximum. Limited liability. It issues a prospectus (a document describing the business, its finances and its risks) before listing. Shareholders elect directors. Directors appoint managers for day-to-day work. An annual report goes to shareholders. A public company is not a government business.

Directors can still face penalties if they break the law, for example by trading while insolvent or by fraud. Limited liability does not protect illegal conduct.

Chapter 2 Government and other structures

Not every organisation is chasing a private profit

Government enterprise

Set up and owned by government to provide a service. Examples: Australia Post, a state water authority. Privatisation is the sale of a government business to private owners, often by listing it. The Commonwealth Bank moved from government ownership to a public company.

Company limited by guarantee

Often a club, charity or association. Members do not take dividends. Surplus is put back into the service. Members have limited liability.

Cooperative

Owned by the people who use it (customers or producers) for mutual benefit. Democratic. Limited liability. Types include consumer, producer and worker cooperatives.

Trust

A trustee holds property for the beneficiaries.

Chapter 2 2.2 Choosing a legal structure

Three factors: size, ownership, finance

Size

Many businesses start as a sole trader. Growth often brings a partnership, then a private company for limited liability, and sometimes a public company if the business wants a very large number of owners.

Ownership

A person who wants full control may stay a sole trader. People who work well in a team may choose a partnership or a private company. Changing structure usually means giving up some control. A members’ club that becomes a shareholder company has demutualised.

Finance

Small businesses often struggle to borrow. New owners bring equity (their money) without a new loan. A public float can raise a large sum, but the original owners share control and must report publicly. Banks may lend more once the business looks established.

Chapter 2 Summary

Chapter summary

  • Classify by size, geography, industry and legal structure.
  • SME means under 200 employees. Large means 200 or more.
  • Sectors: primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary, quinary.
  • Unlimited liability: sole trader and partnership. Limited liability: Pty Ltd and Ltd.
  • Choice of structure depends on size, how much control the owner will share, and where the money will come from.
  • Canva shows an Australian business expanding globally. Aldi shows a global business expanding into Australia.

Chapter 3 3.1 and 3.2 The split

Two sets of influences

  • External: economic, financial, geographic, social, legal, political, institutional, technological, competitive situation, markets.
  • Internal: products, location, resources, management and business culture.
  • External forces happen to the business. Internal forces are choices and features of the business itself.

Example: a rise in interest rates is external. The effect is higher loan costs, or customers spending less.

Chapter 3 Economic influences

The economic cycle

  • The economy is the system of governments, businesses and consumers meeting society’s needs.
  • Upswing: spending, jobs and business investment rise.
  • Boom: spending and employment are high. Prices may rise (inflation). New firms enter.
  • Downswing: spending slows, unsold stock builds, confidence falls.
  • Trough: spending and profits are weak and unemployment is high. Some businesses fail.

Inflation raises the cost of living. Workers ask for higher wages. Higher wages raise business costs. The business may then lift prices or cut staff.

Chapter 3 Three government economic tools

Fiscal, monetary, microeconomic

Fiscal policy

The Commonwealth Budget. Lower taxes or higher government spending can lift demand. Higher taxes or spending cuts can slow it.

Monetary policy

The Reserve Bank sets the cash rate. Higher interest rates make loans dearer, so households and businesses spend less. Lower rates do the opposite.

Microeconomic reform

Changes aimed at one industry so it becomes more competitive: deregulation, removing barriers, or privatising a government business.

Financial influences sit beside these. Global finance, the exchange rate and the availability of credit change how easily a business can borrow and what imports and exports cost.

Chapter 3 Geographic and social

Place and people change demand

Geographic

Australia is in the Asia–Pacific, far from some traditional markets and close to fast-growing Asian economies. Population has shifted rural to urban, inland to the coast, and toward warmer places. Cities that host major events become more visible to global business. Climate decides where farms can operate. Distance raises transport costs.

Social

Businesses must follow community attitudes, tastes and culture. Australia’s population is older, more urban and more culturally mixed. There are more single-parent families. These changes alter what people buy: health services, convenience food, culturally specific products, and environmentally careful brands.

Chapter 3 Legal, political, institutional

Rules, elections, and the bodies that enforce them

  • Legal: laws aim at fair conduct — consumer protection, environment, work health and safety, competition, and workplace relations. A business that ignores them faces fines and reputational harm.
  • Political: an election can change tax, workplace and trade policy. Businesses delay spending when policy is uncertain, and invest when they trust the direction.
  • Institutional: ATO (tax), ASIC (companies), ACCC (competition and consumers), NSW Fair Trading, and the Environment Protection Authority. Also unions, employer associations, the ASX, and consumer groups.

Institutional does not only mean government. A trade union or an employer association can change wages and conditions without being a government department.

Chapter 3 Technology, competition, markets

Tools, rivals, and open markets

Technological

New tools raise productivity and create new products. E-commerce lets a business sell without a shopfront. Technology can also make some jobs redundant.

Competitive situation

Depends on how many rivals there are and how easy it is to enter. Entry is hard if start-up cost is huge or inputs are locked up. Monopoly: one seller. Oligopoly: a few large sellers.

Markets

Deregulation, free-trade agreements and cheaper communication opened Australia to imports and helped exports. Businesses now compete with foreign suppliers as well as local ones.

Chapter 3 Internal influences

Products, location, resources, culture

Products

Goods need production, storage and delivery. Services need to be near customers, and staff need current training. The product decides the process.

Location

Visibility and passing trade matter for shops. Cost of rent matters for everyone. Factories look at inputs, power, water and transport. Some businesses cluster with rivals (fast food, car yards) because shoppers want to compare. Support services — couriers, IT, legal, printing — need to be reachable.

Resources

Human skill, money, equipment, raw materials and information. A shortage inside the business limits what managers can promise customers.

Management and culture

Managers set goals and the way work is done. Culture is “how we do things here”: safety, honesty, how newcomers are treated, whether new ideas are welcome. Culture shows up in quality and in how staff treat customers.

Chapter 3 3.3 Stakeholders

Everyone the decision touches

  • Internal: employees, and the managers who work inside the business.
  • External: customers, suppliers, owners/investors if you are separating them from day-to-day staff, financiers, competitors, community, environment, unions, employer groups.
  • Owners care about profit and the value of their investment. Employees care about pay, safety and job security. Customers care about price, quality and honesty. Suppliers care about being paid on time. The community cares about jobs, tax and harm.

Conflict is normal. A price rise may please owners and upset customers. A cheaper supplier may please finance and upset a community that wanted local purchasing.

Chapter 3 When a company is liquidated

Stakeholders do not lose the same thing

Employees

Jobs end. Wages and entitlements may be unpaid or only partly paid.

Customers

Gift cards, repairs and warranties may be worthless. A needed service disappears.

Suppliers and lenders

Unsecured creditors often receive only some of what they are owed. Secured creditors are in a stronger position.

Shareholders

They are last. Shares can become worthless. They are not usually required to pay company debts beyond the money already invested, because of limited liability.

Community

Local jobs and spending fall. Government may lose tax revenue.

Directors

They must not keep trading if the company is insolvent. Personal penalties can apply if they do.

Liquidation means assets are sold to pay debts and the business is wound up. Employees, customers, suppliers, shareholders and the community each lose something different.

Chapter 3 Summary

Chapter summary

  • External influences are outside the business’s control. Internal influences are features and choices inside it.
  • The economic cycle moves through upswing, boom, downswing and trough.
  • Governments use fiscal policy, monetary policy and microeconomic reform.
  • Geography, society, law, politics, institutions, technology, competition and markets all push from outside.
  • Products, location, resources, management and culture push from inside.
  • Stakeholders are affected differently, and their goals conflict. Liquidation makes those conflicts sharp.

1 mark. Higher interest rates are an external financial influence. Households have less spare money, so restaurant spending falls.

1 mark. Cutting hours is an internal management decision about resources (labour).

1 mark. Employees want hours and income kept. The owner wants lower wage expenses so the shop survives.

1 mark. Those goals conflict: the same decision helps one stakeholder and harms the other.

1 mark. The landlord is an external stakeholder who still expects rent, which adds pressure to cut costs somewhere else.

Chapter 4 · Key words Growth and decline

Key words

Business life cycle
The stages a business, or one of its products, often passes through.
Establishment
The business first enters the market. Costs are high. Sales are slow.
Growth
Sales, profit and market share rise. The business must stay in control of cash and staff.
Maturity
Growth slows. Rivals copy the success. Loyalty and new ideas matter.
Post-maturity
The business then takes a path: steady state, renewal, decline, or cessation.
Steady state
It holds its position and its loyal customers.
Chapter 4 · Key words Growth and decline

Key words

Renewal
New products or a new marketing approach lift sales again.
Decline
Sales and market share fall because challenges were not met.
Cessation
The business closes. Voluntary or involuntary.
Insolvency
The business cannot pay its debts.
Receivership
An independent receiver tries to trade the business out of trouble or sell assets.
Liquidation
Assets are sold, debts are paid as far as the money goes, and the business is wound up.
Chapter 4 4.1 and 4.2 The life cycle

Four stages, then four paths

  • Sales are the usual measure. The cycle can describe one product, not only the whole business.
  • Not every business reaches every stage.
  • Establishment: choose location, product, staff and legal structure. Pay for fit-out and stock before sales begin. Finance is hard because the risk is high. Sales grow slowly.
  • Growth: awareness, revenue, profit and market share rise. Cash flow (liquidity) and specialist staff become the pressure points. Expanding too fast can sink the business.
  • Maturity: market share stops rising quickly. New rivals appear. Profits may still be solid, but standing still is dangerous.
Chapter 4 Post-maturity paths

After maturity, managers choose a direction

Steady state

The business keeps its share and its regular customers. It must still watch rivals, or steady state slides into decline.

Renewal

Sales rise again because of a new product, a new feature, or a fresh price and promotion plan. Example pattern: a phone company that stopped selling home-phone lines and sold data instead.

Decline

Competitive advantage is lost. Sales and market share fall. If this continues, closure becomes likely.

Cessation

The business closes. That may be the owner’s choice, or it may be forced.

Chapter 4 4.3 Challenges and responses

A strategy has to match the stage

Establishment

Challenge: cover costs, find a location, become known, obey registration, tax and safety rules. Response: a tight marketing plan, realistic finance, and legal set-up before opening day.

Growth

Challenge: stockouts or too much stock, weak cash, and owners too busy working in the business. Response: stock control (including just-in-time), budgets, and outsourcing non-core work such as bookkeeping.

Maturity

Challenge: bored customers, bored staff, and new rivals. Response: refresh the product, train and motivate staff, and keep watching competitors’ prices and methods.

Post-maturity

On a renewal path: watch the market and bring staff with the change. On a closure path: meet legal duties to creditors, suppliers and employees.

Just-in-time means stock arrives when it is needed, so less money sits in a storeroom. Outsourcing means another firm does a support task the business used to do itself.

Chapter 4 4.4 Why businesses decline

Decline is usually several failures at once

  • No innovation, so the offer no longer matches what customers want.
  • Weak leadership and no clear plan.
  • Poor cash management: sales on paper, but not enough money to pay bills.
  • Poor inventory: too much unsold stock, or empty shelves.
  • Not enough attention to the customer.
  • Once profits fall, the business may be unable to pay financiers and other creditors (people it owes). Continuing can then be worse than stopping.
Chapter 4 4.5 Voluntary and involuntary cessation

Closing can be a choice or a force

Voluntary — the owner decides

Reasons include retirement, loss of energy, an offer to buy the business, falling profits, or a wish to start something else. Nobody is forcing the closure.

Involuntary — closure is forced

Reasons include the owner’s death, no demand, a weak economy, rivals with much lower costs, or a court order to sell assets for unpaid debt. The most common cause is inability to repay debt: insolvency.

Voluntary: the owner was tired and sold the shop. Involuntary: creditors went to court because bills were unpaid.

Chapter 4 Receivership and liquidation

What happens when the money runs out

  • For a sole trader or partnership, owners are personally responsible for the debts.
  • Receivership: a receiver, an independent manager, is appointed. The receiver may try to keep the business trading, sell it, or cut costs. If that fails, liquidation follows.
  • Liquidation: assets are sold. The cash pays creditors as far as it reaches. For an unincorporated owner, personal assets may still be used. Shareholders in a company usually stop at the money they invested.
  • Bankruptcy is the legal state of being unable to pay debts — more is owed than is owned.
Chapter 4 Summary

Chapter summary

  • Stages: establishment → growth → maturity → post-maturity.
  • Post-maturity paths: steady state, renewal, decline, cessation.
  • Match the response to the stage: cash and awareness early; control and stock in growth; innovation and loyalty in maturity.
  • Decline often comes from no innovation, weak leadership, poor cash, poor stock, and ignoring customers.
  • Voluntary cessation is the owner’s choice. Involuntary cessation is forced, usually by insolvency.
  • Receivership tries to recover the business. Liquidation sells the assets and ends it. Stakeholders lose in different ways.
Chapter 4 Multiple choice

Questions

19. A new mobile-repair van has paid for tools and insurance but still has very few customers. Which stage is this?

  1. Establishment
  2. Maturity
  3. Renewal
  4. Liquidation

A. High set-up costs and slow sales while customers are still learning the business exists are the establishment stage. Renewal is a later path, after maturity.

20. Sales have stopped rising. Two national chains have copied the product. Staff say the work feels routine. Which response fits this stage?

  1. Ignore rivals because market share cannot change
  2. Refresh the product and motivate staff so customers stay
  3. Skip tax registration until growth returns
  4. Appoint a liquidator immediately

B. This is maturity. The fitting strategies are product renewal and keeping staff engaged, while watching competitors. Liquidation is an ending, not the first response to slower growth.

21. The owner of a profitable newsagency retires and sells the business to a family member. This cessation is

  1. involuntary, because a sale occurred
  2. voluntary, because the owner chose to stop
  3. receivership
  4. a monopoly

B. Retirement and a chosen sale are voluntary. Involuntary cessation is forced, for example by unpaid debts and court action. A sale by itself does not make the closure involuntary.

22. A company cannot pay its suppliers. A court appoints an independent manager to see whether the business can be saved or must be sold off. This step is

  1. a prospectus
  2. demutualisation
  3. receivership
  4. fiscal policy

C. Receivership is the appointment of an independent manager when the business is insolvent. If rescue fails, liquidation — selling the assets — is the next step.

Chapter 4 Short answer and case

Short answer and case study

23. Outline two challenges of the growth stage and one way a business can respond to each. (4 marks)

1 mark. Challenge: cash may not keep up with sales. Money is tied up in stock and in customers who pay later.

1 mark. Response: use budgets and watch liquidity so wages, rent and suppliers can still be paid on time.

1 mark. Challenge: stock is hard to judge. Too much does not sell. Too little loses sales.

1 mark. Response: a stock system such as just-in-time, so stock arrives when it is needed.

24. Case study. “Hillside Cycles” has had flat sales for three years. A direct online seller undercuts its prices. The owner has not changed the range since 2019. Cash in the bank is thin, but the business can still pay this month’s bills. Staff numbers are steady.

Identify the stage, explain one factor that could push the business into decline, and recommend one strategy. (6 marks)

1 mark. Flat sales and established rivals point to maturity, not establishment.

1 mark. It is not yet in cessation: bills are still being paid, so it is not insolvent.

1 mark. A decline factor is lack of innovation: the range has not changed since 2019 while customers can buy more cheaply online.

1 mark. A second pressure is the competitive situation, an external influence, which can turn maturity into the decline path.

1 mark. Recommend a renewal strategy: a revised range or a service the website does not offer, such as fitting and repairs, supported by a clear price and promotion plan.

1 mark. Also protect cash while renewing, because thin cash is an early warning of insolvency if sales fall further.

End of Topic 1

Topic 1 in brief

Role

A business satisfies needs and wants. It adds value, and it provides profit, jobs, incomes, choice, innovation, risk-taking, wealth and quality of life.

Types

Classify by size, place, industry and legal structure. The choice of structure depends on size, ownership and finance.

Influences

External influences come from outside. Internal influences come from inside. Stakeholders are helped and hurt in different ways.

Life cycle

Establishment, growth, maturity, then steady state, renewal, decline or cessation. Closure may be voluntary or forced by insolvency, receivership or liquidation.
